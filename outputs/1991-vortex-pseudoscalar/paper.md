# A trapped electric field on an Abelian Higgs vortex

*QCDS-91/S1, November 1991. A paper outside the biennial series.*

## Abstract

Can two transverse waves excite an internal oscillation of a string made of fields? For the unit magnetic vortex in the four-dimensional Abelian Higgs model the answer is yes. In the core the Higgs condensate falls to zero. The longitudinal electric polarization consequently sees an attractive well in two transverse dimensions. We construct a normalizable state below the bulk vector mass and show that its matrix element to two translations is proportional to the integral of its wavefunction against the magnetic flux density. This integral has a definite sign for the lowest state. A second calculation obtains the same matrix element from the charged scalar current. The reflection that preserves the magnetic-flux sector combines a transverse spatial reflection with charge conjugation. The state is odd under this operation and under longitudinal parity, and couples to the antisymmetric right-left phonon pair.

At equal Higgs and vector masses we obtain $m_*/m_V=0.881744$ and $e|Q_{\rm pole}|=1.20101$, where $e$ is the electric charge and $Q_{\rm pole}$ is defined by the normal-curvature vertex of the 1990 note. A parity-even shape oscillation is exactly degenerate with it in the classical fluctuation problem at this coupling. We construct its wavefunction and find an equal pole strength in the trace channel. Thus an unpolarized measurement at this mass would combine two particles. We give the partial width into two translations at weak coupling and a finite-length mixing matrix element. All masses, residues and widths quoted here refer to the magnetic vortex. The calculation gives a microscopic mechanism and a polarization test for a colour flux tube whose dynamics remain to be matched.

## 1. Which question a microscopic tube can settle

Let two waves moving in opposite directions on a straight tube have transverse displacements along axes 1 and 2. In four spacetime dimensions a detector can distinguish a wave on axis 1 arriving from the right from a wave on axis 1 arriving from the left. Antisymmetrizing the two possible assignments makes a state of transverse spin zero. It changes sign under reflection of a transverse axis and under reversal of the direction of the string. A massive particle with the same quantum numbers can appear in this channel. The geometric calculation [1] fixed the projector of its pole. The coefficient multiplying that projector could still vanish, either because the particle was missing from the spectrum or because its matrix element vanished. These are different dynamical possibilities. Solving an eigenvalue problem addresses the first. The second calls for a three-point calculation with normalized external waves.

The choice of microscopic tube matters. A four-dimensional gauge field coupled to a single complex scalar admits a smooth, finite-tension vortex [2]. The electromagnetic penetration length and the length over which the condensate heals can be measured far from the vortex. They also enter the linearized equations near its axis. This gives us two independent checks on a proposed particle. Its frequency must lie on the localized side of the appropriate bulk threshold, and its normalized wavefunction must have a nonzero matrix element to the translations of the whole flux distribution. In this example both questions can be answered using just the scalar amplitude and the azimuthal vector potential of the static solution. No additional pseudoscalar, dielectric function or adjustable core coupling enters the action.

There is a period precedent for the physical polarization. The 1987 calculation of twisted vortices [3] describes a massive photon propagating along a magnetic flux tube. The 1991 analysis [4] explicitly identifies vector bound states and their two-dimensional Proca description. We use the field content and equations available by the end of 1990, give the variational argument in our conventions, and distinguish the contemporary result [4] from our calculation of the polarized residue. The prior observation of a trapped vector is important. It identifies where to look in a theory whose most obvious candidate, the radial Higgs amplitude, is even under both reflections. The actual electric probe, canonical normalization, and overlap with two translation modes still have to be specified to compare with [1].

We work at tree level in the microscopic fields. The straight solution extends through the whole longitudinal direction or winds a periodic spatial direction. There are no ends and no material medium that could absorb longitudinal momentum. At weak gauge coupling one can hold the Higgs-to-vector mass ratio fixed and separate the core frequency from the square root of the tension. A mode below the one-vector threshold can still decay into two massless phonons. The frequency reported from the linear equations and the pole extracted from tree scattering have a common leading value. When discussing a width or a finite-volume splitting we specify the expansion that gives physical meaning to that value. The inequality against the bulk vector threshold alone would not imply an exactly stable quantum particle.

The calculation also gives a way to distinguish a restriction on a residue from a restriction on a spectrum. At the critical coupling an even deformation of the radial profiles has exactly the same frequency as the odd electric oscillation. Its two-phonon source is proportional to the transverse trace. The odd particle has zero residue in the trace even when the full trace amplitude contains a pole at precisely its mass. This feature supplies a direct test of what the channel table measures. It makes critical coupling a useful choice for reasons beyond the simplifying first-order background equations.

## 2. The flux sector, masses and moving coordinates

### 2.1 Normalizations in four dimensions

Take the Minkowski metric $(-,+,+,+)$, transverse Cartesian coordinates $x^1,x^2$, and worldsheet coordinates $\xi^0=t,\xi^1=z$. The flat worldsheet metric is $\eta_{ab}=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1)$. We take $\epsilon_{12}=+1$ in the normal plane and $\varepsilon^{01}=+1$ on the straight sheet. Indices on spacetime tensors are raised with the metric. In particular $F^{0z}=-F_{0z}$. Our microscopic Lagrangian and field strength are

$$
 {\cal L}_4=-\frac14 F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}
 -(D_\mu\Phi)^*D^\mu\Phi-\frac{\lambda}{2}(|\Phi|^2-v^2)^2,\qquad
 D_\mu=\partial_\mu-ie A_\mu,\qquad F=dA .                         \tag{1}
$$

Here $v>0$, $e>0$ and $\lambda>0$. A change $A\mapsto A+d\chi$ multiplies $\Phi$ by $\exp(i e\chi)$. Expanding about the homogeneous vacuum with $\Phi=v+h/\sqrt{2}$ gives a canonically normalized real $h$, a Proca mass term $-e^2v^2 A_\mu A^\mu$, and the vacuum masses

$$
 m_V^2=2e^2v^2,\qquad m_H^2=2\lambda v^2,\qquad
 \beta=\frac{m_H^2}{m_V^2}=\frac{\lambda}{e^2},\qquad \rho=m_V r . \tag{2}
$$

We will vary $\beta$ without changing the definition of the unit of radius. Describing a frequency as a fraction of the Higgs mass would change its dimensionless value by $1/\sqrt{\beta}$. The threshold in the electric eigenvalue problem is the vector mass even on the side with a lighter Higgs particle. The coupling $\beta=1$ will be called critical or Bogomolny coupling. The first-order vortex bound is given in E. B. Bogomolny, *Stability of Classical Solutions*, Sov. J. Nucl. Phys. **24**, 449 (1976). The model (1) contains only bosons. The first-order static equations at this coupling follow by completing squares in an energy.

In regular gauge the positive unit vortex has the profiles

$$
 \Phi_0=v f(\rho)e^{i\theta},\qquad A^{(0)}=\frac{a(\rho)}{e}\,d\theta,\qquad
 B=F^{(0)}_{12}=\frac{m_V^2}{e}\frac{a'(\rho)}{\rho},\qquad
 \int d^2x\,B=\frac{2\pi}{e}.                                      \tag{3}
$$

A prime on a dimensionless profile denotes $d/d\rho$. The physical polar component along a *unit* azimuthal vector is $a/(er)$. In (3) the coefficient of the angular one-form is $a/e$. Confusing these two components changes both the radial equation and the measure in the coupling. Regularity at the origin requires $f(0)=a(0)=0$ with $f=O(\rho)$ and $a=O(\rho^2)$. Finite energy at infinity requires $f,a\to1$. The angular gradient of the scalar phase then cancels the gauge potential in $D_\theta\Phi$, and the circulation of $A$ fixes the flux. Equations (2.8)–(2.13) of [2] give the corresponding flux and radial equations with its charge convention.

The energy per unit length of this configuration is $\sigma=2\pi v^2 \tau$, where

$$
 \tau=\int_0^\infty \rho\,d\rho\left[
 f'^2+\frac{(1-a)^2 f^2}{\rho^2}
 +\frac{a'^2}{\rho^2}+\frac{\beta}{4}(1-f^2)^2\right].             \tag{4}
$$

Stationarity yields the two ordinary differential equations

$$
 \begin{split}
 f''+\rho^{-1} f'-\rho^{-2}(1-a)^2 f+\tfrac{\beta}{2}(1-f^2)f&=0,\\
 a''-\rho^{-1}a'+f^2(1-a)&=0.
 \end{split}                                                        \tag{5}
$$

Throughout we use the ordinary nodeless unit vortex that minimizes the radial energy. The amplitude can be taken nonnegative and is positive for $r>0$. An interior maximum above one contradicts the first equation of (5). The function $1-a$ has positive inner and zero limiting outer boundary values. Its equation $w''-w'/\rho-f^2 w=0$ excludes a negative minimum and a positive interior maximum. It follows also that $(a'/\rho)'=-f^2(1-a)/\rho<0$. Since $a'/\rho$ tends to zero at infinity, $B$ has one strict sign. Positivity of the core overlap later uses this last fact. It would need a new argument for a tube with field reversal or for a radial excitation with nodes.

### 2.2 Tension at the critical point and the translation norm

At $\beta=1$ complete the two squares in (4) using $f'-(1-a)f/\rho$ and $a'/\rho-(1-f^2)/2$. The term left after multiplication by $\rho$ is the derivative of $(1-a) f^2+a$. The integral of that derivative is one. Saturation gives the unit vortex equations

$$
 f'=\frac{(1-a)f}{\rho},\qquad \frac{a'}{\rho}=\frac{1-f^2}{2},
 \qquad \sigma=2\pi v^2=\frac{\pi m_V^2}{e^2}.                     \tag{6}
$$

The equal-mass vortex was investigated in [5]. We also solve the second-order equations (5) at $\beta=1$. Checking (6) on that solution compares two ways of imposing stationarity and fixes the tension used in the residue. At general $\beta$ the positive quantity $\tau$ in (4) is calculated with the actual profiles. Replacing it by one away from the critical point would give the wrong dependence on the bulk mass ratio in a comparison made in tension units.

Let $X^i(t,z)$ be the displacement of the zero of the scalar. A constant $X^i$ moves the whole solution. To leading order the action for a varying displacement is $-\frac12\int d^2\xi\,N_{ij}\partial_aX^i\partial^aX^j$, with

$$
 N_{ij}=\int d^2x\left[F^{(0)}_{ik}F^{(0)}_{jk}
       +2\,\mathop{\rm Re}\big((D_i\Phi_0)^*D_j\Phi_0\big)\right].
                                                                         \tag{7}
$$

This norm can be measured by boosting the tube in a transverse direction. A boost changes its energy per unit coordinate length to $\sigma/\sqrt{1-\dot X^2}$. Its expansion requires $N_{ij}=\sigma\delta_{ij}$. There is a check directly on (4). Angular integration gives $N_{11}=\int d^2x(B^2+\sum_i|D_i\Phi_0|^2)$. Under a change of core radius at fixed flux, magnetic and potential energies scale with opposite powers of the radius, while the transverse covariant-gradient contribution stays constant. Stationarity equates $\int B^2/2$ to $\int \lambda(|\Phi_0|^2-v^2)^2/2$. With this equality, (7) agrees with the energy per length for every $\beta$. We write $y^i=\sqrt{\sigma}\,X^i$ for the canonical, dimensionless worldsheet fields.

There is a reason to retain the expression for $N_{ij}$ before using the virial relation. Moving along a massive vibration takes the profiles away from the static extremum. The derivative of the energy then vanishes at the background, whereas the derivative of the translation norm can be nonzero there. In Section 8 that derivative supplies the vertex of the even partner. Replacing $N_{ij}$ by the stationary energy as a functional identity would lose a physical transition. For the odd mode the same distinction appears in the gauge connection needed to move the vortex.

## 3. An electric degree of freedom and the two reflections

At rest along $z$, impose a linear perturbation $\delta A_z=\psi(r)b_z(t)$, with $\delta\Phi=\delta A_i=\delta A_t=0$. The scalar covariant Laplacian has no term linear in this perturbation on a background with $D_z\Phi_0=0$ and $\partial_z\delta A_z=0$. The transverse Maxwell equations have no source either. The Gauss law at this momentum contains $\partial_z F^{z0}=0$ and vanishes. The $z$ equation does survive. The vortex gives $\delta A_z$ a mass density $2e^2|\Phi_0|^2$ and a transverse gradient energy. Thus the polarization with an electric field parallel to the flux can be studied in an invariant linear subspace of the full equations, without freezing a scalar that it would otherwise source.

A useful Lorentz-covariant version takes $\delta A_a=\psi b_a(\xi)$, $\delta A_i=\delta\Phi=0$ and imposes

$$
 \partial_a b^a=0,\qquad
 H\psi=m_*^2\psi,\qquad
 H=-\nabla_\perp^2+m_V^2 f^2,\qquad
 (\Box_2-m_*^2)b_a=0.                                             \tag{8}
$$

Here $\Box_2=-\partial_t^2+\partial_z^2$. For example, the linear source for transverse $A_i$ contains $\partial_i\psi\,\partial_a b^a$, and the source for the scalar phase contains $\psi\Phi_0\,\partial_a b^a$. Both vanish pointwise. We require $\int d^2x\,\psi^2=1$. Substitution in the quadratic action and integration by parts on the plane give

$$
 {\cal L}_{b}=-\tfrac14 g_{ab}g^{ab}-\tfrac12 m_*^2 b_a b^a,\qquad
 g_{ab}=\partial_a b_b-\partial_b b_a .                            \tag{9}
$$

The divergence condition follows also from the Proca equation $\partial_a g^{ab}=m_*^2 b^b$ for a positive mass. Thus this mode carries one physical oscillator per longitudinal momentum. Boosting a rest solution gives the dispersion $\omega^2=k_z^2+m_*^2$. A boost also produces the time component of $\delta A_a$. Setting that component to zero at all momenta while continuing to set the other fluctuations to zero would in general violate (8).

The physical field is especially transparent at rest. The tensor component $F_{0z}=\psi\,\dot b_z$ is nonzero and unchanged by any four-dimensional gauge transformation. The transverse magnetic perturbation also includes $F_{iz}=(\partial_i\psi)b_z$. Energy alternates between the electric field and its restoring transverse gradient and Higgs contributions, as for a normal oscillator. A gauge function linear in $z$ that removed a spatially constant value of $A_z$ would change other components and the Higgs phase, and would preserve this electric tensor exactly. On a periodic string the same function need not even be single-valued. Gauge fixing cannot eliminate a mode with nonzero field strength and positive quadratic norm. A uniform time-independent Wilson line, if allowed by different boundary conditions, poses a different spectral problem.

For a radial $\psi$ the longitudinal polarization is invariant under rotations of the normal plane. Rotating the background phase in (3) is accompanied by a constant gauge rotation, which acts trivially on $F_{0z}$. Define $P_\parallel$ to reflect $z$ through zero at fixed transverse axes. The background $B=F_{12}$ is preserved. Both $A_z$ at rest and the tensor component $F_{0z}$ change sign. For the independent transverse parity choose the reflection $x^2\mapsto-x^2$. It takes $\theta$ to $-\theta$ and reverses the flux through the normal plane. Charge conjugation $C$ acts by $(\Phi,A)\mapsto(\Phi^*,-A)$. The product $\widetilde P_\perp=C P_\perp$ preserves the positive unit vortex, including its regular gauge representative. The spatial reflection leaves $F_{0z}$ as a tensor component unchanged and $C$ supplies a minus sign. Hence the physical mode has

$$
  J=0,\qquad (\widetilde P_\perp,P_\parallel)=(-,-).               \tag{10}
$$

A bare transverse reflection takes the state to an antivortex. A measurement confined to a single magnetic-flux sector must use the combined operation in (10). Equivalently one can compare vortex and antivortex correlators with the source transformed along with the background. The worldsheet translations are neutral displacements of the core. Under $\widetilde P_\perp$ they transform by the usual reflection matrix on $(X^1,X^2)$ and charge conjugation introduces no extra sign. Under $P_\parallel$ right and left movers exchange. The state $(|1_R2_L\rangle-|2_R1_L\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$ therefore has precisely (10). The combination with the plus sign is a component of a different representation. A sign under both operations makes the particle even under their product. A measurement of simultaneous inversion alone would not resolve the two individual parities.

One can label the oscillator by a canonically normalized pseudoscalar $\varphi$. On the free mass shell choose its sign so that $g^{ab}=m_*\varepsilon^{ab}\varphi$. The Proca equation then determines $b^a$ from one derivative of $\varphi$ divided by $m_*$. At rest the electric fluctuation is one time derivative of a normalized oscillator of frequency $m_*$. This accounts for the factor of mass relating the tensor to $\varphi$. Substituting an on-shell duality equation into a second-order action without transforming its symplectic term can give the wrong sign for the scalar kinetic energy. We use (9) or equivalently its one-particle polarization when normalizing matrix elements. The sign convention for $\varphi$ has no effect on a pole residue.

## 4. Why an eigenstate exists

For angular momentum zero put $\psi=m_V u(\rho)$ and $\mu=m_*/m_V$. The version of (8) to be diagonalized is

$$
 -\frac{1}{\rho}(\rho u')'+f^2u=\mu^2 u,\qquad
 2\pi\int_0^\infty \rho\,u^2\,d\rho=1 .                           \tag{11}
$$

The free operator in the exterior has continuum $\mu^2\geq1$. The difference $f^2-1$ is bounded, vanishes at infinity, and is strictly negative near the axis. A localized multiplication perturbation of this kind leaves the essential spectrum unchanged. One way to see the threshold is to compare exterior domains where $|1-f^2|<\delta$ with free domains of constant potential $1\pm\delta$. The interior regular radial problem has discrete spectrum on every finite disk. Taking the exterior radius to infinity and then $\delta$ to zero fixes the bottom of the essential spectrum. Free packets localized at progressively larger radii supply the continuum above it. A Rayleigh quotient strictly below one consequently gives a discrete eigenvalue on the infinite plane.

The two-dimensional radial measure is decisive. Choose a fixed radius $R_0>0$ with
$J_0=2\pi\int_0^{R_0}\rho(1-f^2)d\rho>0$. A trial function $v_T$ equals one on this disk, falls as $\log(T/\rho)/\log(T/R_0)$ on $R_0<\rho<T$, and vanishes outside. It is continuous with a square-integrable gradient. Its gradient contribution and a bound on its threshold-subtracted potential contribution are

$$
 2\pi\int_0^\infty \rho\,|v_T'|^2\,d\rho=\frac{2\pi}{\log(T/R_0)},\qquad
 2\pi\int_0^\infty \rho(f^2-1)v_T^2\,d\rho\leq-J_0 .             \tag{12}
$$

Taking $T$ large enough makes their sum negative. Dividing by the finite norm of that particular trial function proves binding. No nonzero lower bound on the depth or area of the well had to be supplied. The planar weak-binding phenomenon was analyzed in [6]. The trial here gives the sign needed for the actual vortex at finite couplings. Its large normalization makes it a poor estimator for the binding energy. We use the actual profiles for that estimate. A square well might exhibit the same existence mechanism with a different numerical frequency and coupling.

The unshifted form is $\int d^2\rho\,(|\nabla u|^2+f^2|u|^2)$. A normalizable zero eigenfunction would have to be constant almost everywhere and vanish wherever $f$ is positive. The only such function is zero. As the essential spectrum begins at one and the variational ground eigenvalue lies below one, this also proves strict positivity of that eigenvalue. We have established $0<m_*<m_V$. No additional translational zero mode has been counted. Replacing a real ground function by its absolute value does not increase its form. The regular second-order equation and positivity of the ground state then let us take $u>0$ everywhere in the interior. An interior zero of a nonnegative smooth radial solution would have a zero derivative and imply the identically zero solution. Simplicity in this radial problem follows from uniqueness for the regular initial datum at the axis.

The same argument selects an angular scalar at the bottom of the operator on the plane. In the sector $e^{i\ell\theta}$ the form has the additional nonnegative term $\ell^2|u|^2/\rho^2$. Smoothness at the axis gives the regular radial solution. A singular logarithmic solution in the $\ell=0$ sector has infinite gradient form and is excluded. Our proof gives at least one bound state for every finite positive $\beta$ on the specified unit vortex. Counting higher radial or angular levels requires more information about the well. Existence of a level in this closed linear sector is also independent of where a scalar channel of different parity has its continuum. At $\beta=1/2$ in Section 9 the eigenfrequency even exceeds $m_H$. A claim of binding below every bulk mass would fail at that example.

For $\kappa=\sqrt{1-\mu^2}$ the decaying exterior wave has the form $C K_0(\kappa\rho)$ up to corrections from the exponentially decaying background. Its logarithmic derivative approaches $-\kappa K_1/K_0$. The outer tail, and hence the normalization entering a residue, can penetrate considerably farther than the magnetic flux. Near threshold the two lengths would be parametrically different. Cutting the wave off at a conventional core radius and normalizing only the inner piece would overestimate its matrix element. Our finite-radius calculation matches to the decaying tail and checks its answer against a calculation with a remote reflecting boundary of Dirichlet type.

Finally the binding potential is in a sector with no scalar perturbation. In the homogeneous Higgs phase a vector has transverse and longitudinal polarizations relative to its three-momentum. The name “longitudinal” in this paper specifies the direction of the electric field relative to the *vortex axis* at zero worldsheet momentum. The momenta in the radial wavepacket include transverse momenta. The variable obeying (11) comes from the component with a worldsheet vector index. It cannot be identified by counting the polarizations of a spatially homogeneous plane wave with momentum parallel to the string. Equation (8), the Gauss constraint and the field strength provide a definition throughout the core.

## 5. How two translations excite the electric field

### 5.1 Moving the background connection

We need a three-point matrix element of the original four-dimensional theory. It is sufficient to know three exact linearized solutions and any smooth embedding of those solutions into trial fields with the correct first variations. To see this, write the trial field as the background plus a linear part $\delta_1$ and a quadratic part $\delta_2$ in external amplitudes. In the cubic action a change of $\delta_2$ is paired by the quadratic fluctuation operator with $\delta_1$. Integration by parts makes it proportional to the free equations of the external legs. A change at third order multiplies the background equation. Both vanish on shell, with wavepackets or the usual scattering boundary conditions. The cubic functional evaluated on the external solutions is consequently invariant under these changes. This argument requires the full linearized solution, including the gauge constraint. At fourth order the responses of other fields to a quadratic source can contribute.

Take translated coordinates $U^i=x^i-X^i(\xi)$. A useful trial configuration is

$$
 \Phi=\Phi_0(U),\qquad
 A=A_i^{(0)}(U)\,dU^i+\psi(U)b_a(\xi)d\xi^a,\qquad
 dU^i=dx^i-\partial_aX^i d\xi^a .                                  \tag{13}
$$

In particular, the translated background already has a component $-A_i^{(0)}(U)\partial_aX^i$ along the sheet. Transporting the scalar and only the two transverse Cartesian components of the potential would omit this term. The pullback in (13) guarantees that the field strength of a translated flux distribution transforms as a two-form. It is also gauge covariant. A gauge transformation of the background by $\chi(U)$ adds the full exact differential, including its sheet part.

At first order in $X$ the perturbation in (13) is a translation plus a convenient choice of gauge. A gauge change with parameter $X^i A_i^{(0)}(x)$ puts it in the form $\delta\Phi=-X^i D_i\Phi_0$, $\delta A_j=-X^iF^{(0)}_{ij}$ and $\delta A_a=0$. Differentiating the static equations gives the translation zero modes. Their background-gauge constraint follows from the static Maxwell equation as we verify below. Multiplying by a worldsheet wave with $\Box_2 X^i=0$ then solves the complete linearized equations. The second external solution, the part linear in $b$, is exactly (8). Thus (13) meets the on-shell requirement even at a phonon energy of $m_*/2$. We have not expanded any propagator in the ratio of that energy to the core mass.

Translate the transverse integration variable after taking derivatives at fixed $x$. Its Jacobian on each sheet is unity. In Cartesian components the identities following from (13) include

$$
 \begin{split}
 D_a\Phi&=-\partial_a X^i D_i\Phi_0-ie\psi b_a\Phi_0,\\
 F_{ai}&=-\partial_aX^j F^{(0)}_{ji}-(\partial_i\psi)b_a,\\
 F_{ab}&= B\,\epsilon_{ij}\partial_aX^i\partial_bX^j+\psi g_{ab}
    -(\partial_i\psi)(\partial_aX^i b_b-\partial_bX^i b_a).
 \end{split}                                                        \tag{14}
$$

The transverse components retain their background values at the translated point. With the original flat Cartesian metric the terms $|D_a\Phi|^2$ and $F_{ai}F^{ai}$ are quadratic polynomials in $b$ and $\partial X$ after this translation of the integral. A cross term linear in a single transverse vector vanishes by rotations. There is no term there with one $b$ and two translations. The quadratic-in-$X$ and linear-in-$b$ pieces of $F_{ab}$ do multiply in the Maxwell action. The last term of (14) starts with one of each external field and would need a zeroth- or linear-in-$X$ sheet field strength to contribute to $bXX$. The background supplies neither. We find the entire on-shell cubic vertex in this representation from one cross term,

$$
 \mathcal L_{bXX}=-\frac{\mathcal I}{2}\,g^{ab}\epsilon_{ij}
                \partial_a X^i\partial_b X^j,\qquad
 \mathcal I=\int d^2x\,B(x)\psi(x).                                \tag{15}
$$

Both the factor $1/2$ and the two distinct assignments of the external phonon fields will matter for the residue. The Maxwell normalization in (1) gives the first factor. It comes from differentiating a square with two different terms. The assignments of the phonon fields still have to be made in (15). Physically, when the transverse velocity and slope of a flux distribution point in independent directions, the two-form carried by the moving distribution acquires a sheet component. An electric oscillation with weight $\psi$ detects this component. Its transverse integral measures the distribution of flux, even though the total flux is quantized.

The integral $\mathcal I$ has mass dimension one. The canonical two-dimensional vector $b_a$ has dimension zero, $\psi$ dimension one, and $B$ dimension two. Quantized flux and a unit-normalized eigenfunction alone would not fix this integral. Here $B>0$ and the lowest $\psi$ can be chosen strictly positive, so $\mathcal I>0$. A second radial level with a node might have a very small or a zero overlap even if its eigenvalue were below threshold. It is the definite sign of the ground-state overlap that turns existence into a prediction of scattering. The sign of one external massive field can be reversed by convention. The interference sign between two ordered external polarizations will be fixed by their relative orientation.

### 5.2 A second calculation in background gauge

The previous derivation obtained the interaction from a Maxwell term, although the Abelian Maxwell action is exactly quadratic in Cartesian field fluctuations. A second choice of coordinates resolves this apparent puzzle. In coordinates linear in the perturbations, the same on-shell amplitude comes from the matter current. This probes factors of charge, the compensating gauge transformation and the treatment of longitudinal fields. Only the projected result needs to agree between the two representations.

Use the translation gauge stated after (13). Put $d_i=D_i\Phi_0$ and vary $\Phi$ by $-X^i d_i$ and $A_j$ by $-X^i F^{(0)}_{ij}$. The current convention from (1) gives $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=-2e\,\mathop{\rm Im}(\Phi^*D^\nu\Phi)$. For $\delta A_a=0$ the left side of the sheet Maxwell equation of a moving translation is $\partial^a X^j\,\partial_i F_{ji}^{(0)}$. The right side is $2e\,\partial^a X^j\,\mathop{\rm Im}(\Phi_0^*D_j\Phi_0)$. These agree by the static equation and antisymmetry of $F$. The scalar and transverse Maxwell equations likewise reduce to the static zero mode times $X$ plus terms proportional to $\Box_2 X$. This checks the constraint for both a left and a right mover without assuming equal energies.

In these linear variables put $\delta A_a=\psi b_a$ for the massive polarization. Neither the Maxwell term nor the scalar potential has an elementary $bXX$ term. Expanding the part of the scalar action with a sheet derivative gives the cubic current

$$
 \mathcal L^{\rm lin}_{bXX}=2e\!\int d^2x\,\psi(x)\,K(x)\,
    b^a\epsilon_{ij}X^i\partial_a X^j,\qquad
 K=\mathop{\rm Im}\big[(D_1\Phi_0)^*D_2\Phi_0\big].               \tag{16}
$$

For instance, the coefficient of a single $b_a$ in $-|D_a\Phi|^2$ is $2e\psi\,\mathop{\rm Im}(\Phi^*\partial^a\Phi)$. Inserting the two displaced scalars gives (16). In these coordinates a constant center shift is realized beyond first order by terms outside this linear parametrization. The displayed bare $X$ is consistent with that realization of translations. On shell it gives the same soft and parity properties as (15).

There is a differential identity connecting the two weight functions. Write $f_r=df(m_Vr)/dr$ and $w=1-a(m_Vr)$. On the positive unit solution, $K=v^2 f_r w f/r$ and $dB/dr=-2e v^2 w f^2/r$ by (5). Differentiate the second expression, add the radial divergence term and use $dw/dr=-erB$. The terms involving $dw/dr$ give $2e^2v^2 f^2 B$. The terms involving $f_r$ give $-4eK$. Thus

$$
 H B=4eK,\qquad
 4e\int d^2x\,\psi K=m_*^2\int d^2x\,B\psi=m_*^2\mathcal I.       \tag{17}
$$

The last equality uses self-adjointness and (8). At the axis $r(\psi B'-B\psi')$ vanishes by regularity, and at infinity both profiles decay. This proves a current sum rule for the actual vortex, independent of critical coupling. It relates a matrix element weighted by a gradient of the scalar to one weighted by the magnetic field. The checks with these two integrands in Section 9 also probe the numerical background.

A direct check on the sign and factor of two is available without choosing plane-wave phases. Put $J_b=\epsilon_{ij}X^i\partial_bX^j$. Then $g^{ab}\partial_aJ_b=g^{ab}\epsilon_{ij}\partial_aX^i\partial_bX^j$. Integrating (15) by parts and using $\partial_a g^{ab}=m_*^2 b^b$ gives $+m_*^2\mathcal I\, b^a J_a/2$. This is exactly (16) after (17). Equality uses the massive external equation, so the two vertices proportional to $g^{ab}$ and $b^a$ need not give the same numerator for an off-shell exchange diagram. Contact interactions transform along with the field variables. The value at the pole and the rank of its residue do agree.

## 6. Matching the geometric vertex at its pole

Fix the normal-curvature convention used in [1]. For an oriented orthonormal pair of normals the connection is $\mathcal A_a=n_1\cdot\partial_a n_2$. In flat ambient space its curvature is $\mathcal F_{ab}=\epsilon_{ij}K^i_{ac}h^{cd}K^j_{bd}$, with $h$ the induced metric and $K^i$ the second fundamental forms. The geometric interaction is $\sqrt{-h}\,Q\varphi\,\varepsilon^{ab}\mathcal F_{ab}$ where the curved $\varepsilon^{01}=1/\sqrt{-h}$. The earlier discussion of the normal-bundle density [7] concerns a parity-sensitive term of a surface. A constant multiplying the integral of a curvature drops out of perturbative bulk scattering. The dynamical external state and its normalization in (9) are essential here.

To lowest order $K^i_{ab}=\partial_a\partial_b y^i/\sqrt{\sigma}$. With both phonon momenta incoming the geometry gives $V_{ij}^{\rm geom}=2Q\epsilon_{ij}(p\cdot q)(p\wedge q)/\sigma$, where $p\wedge q=\varepsilon^{ab}p_a q_b$. For a right wave $p^a=(E_R,E_R)$ and a left wave $q^a=(E_L,-E_L)$ we have $s=-(p+q)^2=4E_RE_L$, $p\cdot q=-s/2$ and $p\wedge q=s/2$. Differentiating (15) twice with respect to the phonons gives two equal contributions. With the freely chosen sign of the electric polarization the comparison of magnitudes is

$$
 |V^{\rm vortex}_{12}|_{s=m_*^2}
    =\frac{m_*|\mathcal I|}{\sigma}|p\wedge q|
    =\frac{|\mathcal I|m_*^3}{2\sigma},\qquad
 |V^{\rm geom}_{12}|_{s=m_*^2}=\frac{|Q|m_*^4}{2\sigma}.
                                                                         \tag{18}
$$

The sign for $21$ is opposite to that for $12$ with the momenta still ordered right then left. Interchanging the complete boson legs reverses the wedge at the same time as $\epsilon_{ij}$, so Bose symmetry holds. Parallel polarizations give zero for an odd source. Copropagating momenta also have zero wedge and cannot produce the on-shell particle. In the rest frame each phonon has energy $m_*/2$ and the momenta have opposite signs, giving the same invariant in (18).

We define $Q_{\rm pole}$ by this on-shell comparison. The dimensionless coupling obeys

$$
 |Q_{\rm pole}|=\frac{|\mathcal I|}{m_*}.                            \tag{19}
$$

This determines a physical tree residue even when the core frequency is so close to its inverse size that an expansion about $s=0$ is inaccurate at the pole. It does not uniquely assign the coefficient of one off-shell operator in every description of the tube. A momentum-dependent scalar vertex can be written as its value on shell plus $(s-m_*^2)$ times a regular function. The extra terms become regular pieces in a tree exchange. The finite part of a four-phonon amplitude also has independent contact and crossed contributions, including those of other modes of (1). Determining all of them requires a different matching calculation. None can remove a simple isolated pole with a nonzero projected matrix element.

One can see the relation of derivative orders locally on external solutions. Write $W=\epsilon_{ij}\varepsilon^{ab}\partial_a y^i\partial_b y^j$. Free massless propagation gives

$$
 \Box_2 W=2\epsilon_{ij}\varepsilon^{ab}
     \partial_c\partial_a y^i\partial^c\partial_b y^j
 \quad \text{when }\Box_2 y^i=0 .                                 \tag{20}
$$

Between two on-shell phonons and an on-shell scalar of mass $m_*$, integration by parts replaces $\varphi W$ by $\varphi\,\Box_2W/m_*^2$. The dual of (15) has coefficient of magnitude $m_*|\mathcal I|/(2\sigma)$ multiplying $\varphi W$. Equation (20) produces the two second fundamental forms with precisely the magnitude $|\mathcal I|/(m_*\sigma)$. This step divides by a nonzero eigenmass and applies at three points. Extrapolation to a massless compact coordinate or a soft off-shell background would require additional information.

Use $S=\mathbf 1+i\mathcal M/(2s)+\cdots$ on ordered right-left states and define positive tree pole strength by $R=\lim_{s\to m_*^2}(m_*^2-s)\mathcal M$. Our $R$ is the negative of the Laurent residue in $s$. The electric-particle contribution factorizes as

$$
 R^{\rm odd}_{ij,kl}=g_*^2\,\epsilon_{ij}\epsilon_{kl},\qquad
 g_*=\frac{|\mathcal I|m_*^3}{2\sigma},\qquad
 R_A=2g_*^2=\frac{Q_{\rm pole}^2 m_*^8}{2\sigma^2}.               \tag{21}
$$

Here $g_*$ is the ordered on-shell matrix element and has mass dimension two. It differs from the dimensionless $Q_{\rm pole}$ and the microscopic gauge charge $e$. For a normalized tensor $T_{ij}$ of incoming polarizations the strength is $g_*^2|T_{12}-T_{21}|^2$. A classical pair of waves with real linear polarization vectors $l,r$ sources an amplitude proportional to the signed area $\epsilon_{ij}r^i l^j$. Squaring before antisymmetrizing would lose the interference which distinguishes the spin-zero channels. The crossed terms of this massive exchange are regular at positive $s=m_*^2$.

## 7. Flux moments and normalization of the matching data

Equation (19) can be evaluated without a scattering simulation. Define $\alpha=e\mathcal I/m_V$ with the positive ground-state convention. In terms of (3) and (11),

$$
 \alpha=2\pi\int_0^\infty a' u\,d\rho,\qquad
 e|Q_{\rm pole}|=\frac{\alpha}{\mu},\qquad
 \frac{m_*}{\sqrt{\sigma}}=\frac{e\mu}{\sqrt{\pi\tau}} .           \tag{22}
$$

There is no extra power of $\rho$ in the first expression. The area measure cancels the $1/\rho$ in $B$. An integration of $u$ weighted with the uncorrected vector potential $a$, which tends to a constant, would include an unphysical exterior contribution. In (22) only the region where flux passes through the transverse plane can act as a source. A normalized state extending mostly beyond this region would couple weakly. The factor of $1/\mu$ in $Q_{\rm pole}$ comes from comparing to a curvature vertex with four external momenta.

There are two integral checks with distinct physical meanings. First, integrating (11) over the plane gives

$$
 \int d^2\rho\,(1-f^2)u=(1-\mu^2)\int d^2\rho\,u.                 \tag{23}
$$

The surface term is $2\pi\rho u'$ at the two boundaries and vanishes for the regular decaying eigenfunction. The integrals involve the first power of the wave. They are particularly sensitive to how its far tail is continued. At critical coupling (6) makes the left side $2\alpha$. There is also a norm inequality. With the normalization in (11), $\mathcal I^2\leq\int B^2d^2x$. Equality would require $B$ to be proportional to the eigenfunction. At $\beta=1$, $B$ decays with vector scale whereas $u$ decays with the smaller inverse length $\kappa m_V$, so equality cannot hold. The overlap measures how much of a magnetic source lies in the discrete electric mode. Other spectral weight can sit in additional states and the continuum.

Second, the current sum rule (17) in dimensionless units reads

$$
 4\pi\int_0^\infty u f'(1-a)f\,d\rho=\mu^2\alpha .                \tag{24}
$$

This involves profile and derivative weights different from (23). Near the axis the magnetic integrand is proportional to $\rho$, with coefficient fixed by the core magnetic field. The current integrand is proportional to $\rho$ there too, with coefficient fixed by the slope of the Higgs amplitude. Those local coefficients need not agree pointwise. Equation (24) is an equality of integrated on-shell matrix elements. Changing the mass unit independently in the two calculations would spoil this test even with smooth profiles.

At a specified $\beta$ the microscopic gauge charge drops out of the product

$$
 |Q_{\rm pole}|\,\frac{m_*}{\sqrt{\sigma}}
       =\frac{\mathcal I}{\sqrt{\sigma}}
       =\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{\pi\tau}} .                            \tag{25}
$$

Measuring $\sigma$ and the two vacuum masses fixes $e$ (at tree level through $\tau$) and $\beta$. The bound-state frequency and its coupling can then be predicted without fitting the electric peak. Alternatively, fitting $e$ with the odd mass at fixed $\sigma,\beta$ leaves (25) to predict the residue. The product is a test across this family of vortices. Flux quantization does not fix it, and we shall see it change with $\beta$. In a theory with more matter, additional profiles or physical polarizations could enter this parameter count.

## 8. A degenerate scalar and the meaning of the zero

### 8.1 Constructing the companion state

A search for a pole at a prescribed frequency can confuse a scalar deformation of the core with an electric oscillation. At $\beta=1$ this is an exact ambiguity if the experiment discards the reflections. We can build the scalar state directly from the solution of (11). It varies the amplitude $f$ and the azimuthal gauge profile $a$ while keeping $A_a=0$. Both variations preserve the axial symmetry and are even under $\widetilde P_\perp$ and $P_\parallel$. For a varying worldsheet coefficient the scalar variation is in phase with $\Phi_0$, hence gives no linear sheet current. The azimuthal transverse vector is divergence free. The sheet component of the Maxwell constraint is satisfied in this sector as well.

Write the infinitesimal variations as $\delta f=\eta$ and $\delta a=\rho c$. The kinetic norm, before choosing a normalization of the mode, is proportional to $\int d^2\rho\,(\eta^2+c^2)$. This equality of weights follows from the vector mass convention in (2) and the use of the azimuthal component along a unit vector. The static Hessian follows by linearizing the two squares leading to (6). In the radial space with measure $2\pi\rho d\rho$ put $s_0=(1-a)/\rho$ and denote the first-order operator by $D$,

$$
 D\binom{\eta}{c}=
 \binom{(\partial_\rho-s_0)\eta+f c}{f\eta+(\partial_\rho+1/\rho)c},
 \qquad
 D^\dagger\binom{0}{u}=\binom{f u}{-u'} .                         \tag{26}
$$

The surface term in the energy is constant for variations regular at the axis and decaying at infinity. The radial mass-squared operator in units of $m_V^2$ is therefore $D^\dagger D$. The adjoint is with the stated radial measure, for which $(\partial_\rho)^\dagger=-\partial_\rho-1/\rho$. Acting on the particular vector in (26) gives
$D D^\dagger(0,u)=( (f'-s_0 f)u,\,-u''-u'/\rho+f^2u)$. The first component vanishes by the background equation (6). The second is exactly $\mu^2 u$. Acting once more with $D^\dagger$ then proves that $(fu,-u')$ is an eigenvector of the scalar Hessian with eigenvalue $\mu^2$. This is an equality of differential operators restricted to the specified subspace. No numerical coincidence is being inferred from rounding frequencies.

The partner is regular. Near the axis $fu=O(\rho)$ and $u'=O(\rho)$, so its $\delta a=O(\rho^2)$. In the exterior the tail is square integrable, and its flux change, proportional to $\delta a(\infty)-\delta a(0)$, vanishes. For a positive eigenvalue the map $D^\dagger$ cannot annihilate $(0,u)$ since its squared norm is $\langle (0,u),DD^\dagger(0,u)\rangle=\mu^2$. There is a genuine scalar excitation that changes the magnitude of the condensate in the core. Its value is directly visible in the gauge-invariant $|\Phi|^2$ away from the axis. The odd electric mode has zero linear change in this operator. Conversely the scalar gives zero linear $F_{0z}$. These two observations distinguish the particles even at the critical point.

For an explicit canonical scalar $h_s(\xi)$ choose

$$
  \delta f=\frac{e}{\mu} f u\,h_s,\qquad
  \frac{\delta a}{\rho}=-\frac{e}{\mu}u' h_s.                     \tag{27}
$$

Indeed the microscopic coefficient of minus the squared sheet gradient of this variation, with the factor $1/2$ extracted, is $(1/e^2)\int d^2\rho\,[(\partial_{h_s}f)^2+(\partial_{h_s}a/\rho)^2]$. The integral of $f^2u^2+u'^2$ is $\mu^2$ by (11). The normalization in (27) then gives the canonical term $-(\partial h_s)^2/2$. The scalar and vector involve the same radial eigenfunction but different four-dimensional fluctuating fields. The construction uses the first-order equations. Away from critical coupling the odd mode still satisfies (11) and (15), while the first component of $DD^\dagger(0,u)$ need no longer vanish and the two-square factorization itself changes.

### 8.2 What the scalar contributes to the trace

To calculate the second column of pole strengths in Section 6, move the vortex as in (13) and vary the static profiles along (27). The term quadratic in $\partial X$ is $-N(h_s)(\partial X)^2/2$ with $N(0)=\sigma$. Rotational invariance makes $N_{ij}=N\delta_{ij}$ at every point on this radial path. Terms with a single transverse displacement and a derivative of $h_s$ integrate to zero by rotations. The sheet field strength of a static radial profile has no linear term in $h_s$. Direct expansion thus gives the cubic interaction $-N'(0)h_s(\partial y)^2/(2\sigma)$, where here the prime means differentiation along the profile path with respect to the canonical $h_s$. The same on-shell argument used for (13) allows this continuation away from the static solution. In particular it keeps the displaced even mode tangent to the exact linearized solution.

To evaluate $N'(0)$ write $E_B=\int B^2 d^2x/2$ and $E_U=\int U d^2x$ with $U=\lambda(|\Phi|^2-v^2)^2/2$. For a rotationally symmetric path through the vortex,
$N=E+E_B-E_U$. The energy $E$ is stationary at the vortex for a variation of fixed flux. At critical coupling introduce $B_{\rm eq}=e v^2(1-f^2)$, evaluated using the varied scalar profile. The potential is identically $B_{\rm eq}^2/2$ there and $B=B_{\rm eq}$ on the background. It follows that

$$
 N'(0)=\int d^2x\,B\,\partial_{h_s}(B-B_{\rm eq})\big|_0
       =m_*\mathcal I .                                             \tag{28}
$$

For the last equality note that the variation of $e(B-B_{\rm eq})/m_V^2$ is the second component of $D(\eta,c)$. Equations (26) and (27) give this component as $e\mu u$ per unit $h_s$. Since $\psi=m_V u$, the physical variation of $B-B_{\rm eq}$ is $m_*\psi$. The dimensions of (28) agree on both sides. $N$ has the dimension of tension, $h_s$ has dimension zero on the sheet, and the flux overlap has dimension one.

The ordered scalar matrix element is $(N'(0)/\sigma)(p\cdot q)\delta_{ij}$ up to a field sign. Its magnitude per diagonal entry at the pole is $m_*^3|\mathcal I|/(2\sigma)=g_*$. Since the trace tensor has norm $\sqrt{2}$, the projected strength is $2g_*^2=R_A$. Even and odd amplitudes cannot mix linearly while both reflection symmetries hold. A detector which measures a line at $\mu^2\simeq0.77747$ on a critical vortex should therefore analyze its source. The antisymmetrization that isolates the vector field kills the companion scalar. A trace source sees the scalar with the same leading mass and strength. Radiative corrections in the purely bosonic theory are not constrained by the classical square completion to keep this degeneracy. We use it only at the order at which (26) was derived.

## 10. What a scattering or finite-length experiment would see

### 10.1 Parameter-free combinations and a partial width

Consider fixing $e$ and the two vacuum masses by measurements in the homogeneous phase. At critical coupling the magnetic-vortex predictions from (21)–(25) become

$$
 \begin{split}
 m_*/\sqrt{\sigma}&=0.497471\,e,\qquad
 |Q_{\rm pole}|=1.20101/e,\\
 |Q_{\rm pole}|m_*/\sqrt{\sigma}&=0.597469,\qquad
 R_A/\sigma^2=0.00270524\,e^6.
 \end{split}                                                        \tag{29}
$$

Even knowing $\sigma$ alone at this point gives no absolute prediction for $m_*/\sqrt{\sigma}$ because $e$ can vary while the tension stays fixed. The product in the second line is stronger. It leaves no continuously adjustable parameter at specified $\beta$. If one fixes $e$ from the rest frequency, that same choice must account for the response to a pair of translations. At $\beta=1/2$ and $2$ the analogous products are $0.606981$ and $0.560805$. Thus the critical value has a microscopic meaning which is lost if the mass and coupling of an effective pseudoscalar are fitted independently at every point.

We can quantify the distinction between localization and stability. Use relativistic normalization $\langle p'|p\rangle=2E(2\pi)\delta(p-p')$ for each one-dimensional outgoing phonon. In the rest frame the odd mode decays into one phonon of each species. Label one by its species and integrate over both signs of its momentum. Energy conservation gives $|p|=m_*/2$. After the spatial momentum delta function is used, the two-body phase space is
$\int dp\,\delta(m_*-2|p|)/(4p^2)=1/m_*^2$. There is no factorial for distinct species. Equivalently one can sum over ordered species and use the identical-boson completeness factor $1/2$. Multiplying by $g_*^2/(2m_*)$ gives the leading partial width

$$
 \Gamma_{12}=\frac{g_*^2}{2m_*^3}
        =\frac{R_A}{4m_*^3}
        =\frac{Q_{\rm pole}^2 m_*^5}{8\sigma^2}.
                                                                    \tag{30}
$$

At critical coupling this is $\Gamma_{12}/m_*=0.0110427\,e^2$ at leading order. A large $|Q_{\rm pole}|$ at small $e$ is compatible with a narrow mode. The on-shell expansion parameter involves also the phonon momenta in units of $\sqrt{\sigma}$. In fact $|Q_{\rm pole}|m_*^2/\sigma$ is of order $e$ here. Formula (30) uses the tree matrix element in the leading decay probability. A real isolated pole of the linear radial resolvent turns into a resonance in interacting phonon scattering on an infinite tube. The width quoted is into the two specified translations. Higher processes, mass shifts, and a full quantum line shape have not been calculated. As $e$ tends to zero with $\beta$ fixed, the classical mass ratios and product in (25) have a weak-coupling meaning.

The critical partner in Section 8 gives a further check on the meaning of a projected residue. It can decay to $11$ or $22$, with ordered amplitude of magnitude $g_*$ in each case. For either pair the integral over both directions of the momentum now counts the same final state twice. Its identical-particle factor gives $\Gamma_{11}^{\rm even}=\Gamma_{22}^{\rm even}=g_*^2/(4m_*^3)$. Adding the two yields $\Gamma_{11}^{\rm even}+\Gamma_{22}^{\rm even}=\Gamma_{12}^{\rm odd}$ at this order. Thus the two particles have the same leading partial width into the complete translation sector, as well as the same critical eigenmass and projected pole strength. The final species and the interference under interchange distinguish them. Equality of their total lifetimes including any other decay channels would require more information.

A gauge-invariant source for a direct measurement is $\int d^2x\,B(x)F_{0z}(t,z,x)$ with the transverse weight taken from the static vortex. If the tube can wander, define the weight relative to the measured center or prepare a definite transverse momentum and separate center motion. The one-particle matrix element of this source has magnitude $m_*|\mathcal I|$ in canonical relativistic normalization. Its two-point function can contain other poles or a continuum, but its projected tree pole gives precisely the overlap needed in (15). The source linear in $|\Phi|^2-|\Phi_0|^2$ with an even radial weight detects the scalar partner instead. Combining an electric source with a pair of directed displacement sources tests the *sign change* upon interchanging transverse indices. A static histogram of the core radius would chiefly see the even particle.

### 10.2 A test on a periodic tube

Another consequence can be obtained without any off-shell effective action at fourth order. Compactify $z$ with circumference $L$ and choose a tube winding that flat direction. There is no background bending energy. Work in its zero longitudinal momentum sector, remove center-of-mass translation, and let $k=2\pi n/L$ for a positive integer $n$. The free odd oscillator at rest has energy $m_*$ above the tube vacuum. The two-phonon states with momenta $k,-k$ have energy $2k$. At $L_n=4\pi n/m_*$ they cross at leading order. Restrict first to the one-particle and normalized antisymmetric two-phonon states. The matrix element that lifts their crossing is fixed by (18).

Each finite-volume external leg contributes $1/\sqrt{2E L}$ relative to the invariant amplitude, and the spatial integral of the interaction gives $L$ for zero total momentum. The two ordered species assignments have opposite signs and add with a factor $\sqrt{2}$ in the antisymmetric state. At the unperturbed crossing we thus obtain

$$
 |v_A(L_n)|=
 \frac{\sqrt{2}\,g_* L_n}{\sqrt{2m_*L_n}\,(2kL_n)}
 =\frac{g_*}{m_*\sqrt{m_*L_n}},\qquad k=m_*/2 .                   \tag{31}
$$

In an isolated two-state crossing the minimum level separation is $2|v_A|$ to leading order. At the first critical crossing $m_*L_1=4\pi$ its ratio to $m_*$ is $0.08385\,e$. At the same order the electric oscillator has zero mixing with either of the normalized spin-two states or the trace combination. The even scalar can have its own crossing in the trace at critical coupling, with the same gap from (28). A two-source measurement at fixed reflection parity would exhibit the two otherwise indistinguishable rest energies separately.

Several limits enter this prediction. The first crossing is particularly simple because in the free zero-momentum two-translation sector the first excited left and right partitions each consist of a single quantum. For larger $n$ more phonon partitions are exactly degenerate in the free theory. Equation (31) is still a matrix element of a specified pair of states, while the gaps require diagonalizing the relevant degenerate subspace. Even at $n=1$, if other states are present in the same symmetry block within the splitting scale, the two-state formula needs enlargement. Diagonal phonon interactions and quantum corrections shift the location of an isolated crossing. At fixed $n$ and weak $e$ these are higher order than the order-$e$ mixing relative to $m_*$, under the usual semiclassical expansion around this vortex. The sheet dispersion and three-point projection used in (31) do not require the phonon momentum to be small compared to $m_*$ at the crossing. The limit $L\sqrt{\sigma}\gg1$ holds there if $e$ is small, since $m_*/\sqrt{\sigma}$ is then of order $e$.

One can check the normalization of the width by letting $L$ grow. At zero total momentum the density of right-left pair energies in the antisymmetric channel is $L/(4\pi)$. Evaluating (31) at resonance gives $2\pi |v_A|^2 L/(4\pi)=g_*^2/(2m_*^3)$, the phase-space answer (30). This consistency test includes both the normalization of the antisymmetric tensor and the factor counting the directions of the waves. Treating an ordered $12$ state as the entire normalized channel in one calculation and summing both directions again in the other would give two inconsistent widths. Using the odd one-particle pole as a strictly stable finite-volume level at arbitrarily large $L$ would also miss these accumulating crossings.

## 11. Scope of the result

A gauge field whose vacuum mass is supplied by a condensate can lose part of its restoring force inside a defect. For the longitudinal electric polarization of the unit vortex the local vector mass squared is proportional to $f^2$. The transverse kinetic energy is that of a regular radial wave in two dimensions, which permits an arbitrarily weak attractive well to bind. The ground state has one sign. The magnetic flux also has one sign. This pair of properties supplies both the bound state and the nonzero residue. A topology argument for the flux alone would have given neither the eigenfrequency nor the matrix element. Conversely, a variational proof of the frequency inequality without the sign and normalization of the source would have left open the possibility that translations could never excite it.

The assumptions can be tested at different stages. A measured linear frequency above the vector continuum would contradict the assignment to the lowest solution of (11) for this model. An electric normal mode with a vanishing antisymmetric overlap would contradict (15) and (17) on a nodeless unit vortex with the canonical action. At fixed $\beta=1$ a discrepancy with the product in (29) after controlling finite-volume and quantum corrections would distinguish a different inner profile or microscopic kinetic term from a simple change of $e$. A trace line at the odd mass is explicitly predicted at critical coupling by (26)–(28). Rejecting the pseudoscalar on the basis of that line alone would identify a mass without identifying its particle. These are possible independent measurements on the same theory with parameters set by bulk or static data.

Extension to a different flux tube requires identifying its field that plays the role of $F_{0z}$ and the discrete operation preserving its oriented sector. A Wilson line for an electric charge in the Higgs phase of (1) is screened. This vortex carries magnetic flux and can confine external magnetic probes in a corresponding setup. A colour-electric confining string requires a different microscopic dictionary even if the low-energy geometric interaction looks the same. Extra light matter, an additional phase of a condensate, or a different realization of reflection can change the radial equations and their source. The critical scalar partner is particularly sensitive to the first-order structure of the static energy. The pole data in (29) are benchmarks for such a comparison. Obtaining the tension and a polarized excitation of a colour tube from a single microscopic construction remains the main programme.

## Appendix. Historical inputs and memory

**Period results rederived.** The unit local vortex, its equal-mass first-order background and the flux-twist interpretation are available before the end of 1990 [2,3,5]. The contemporaneous 1991 article [4] explicitly obtains the planar binding problem and the Proca polarization. We credit that result independently of the chronology of this exercise. Its discussion of the additive constant of a scalar potential for a divergence-free vector does not change the normalization in (18). We use the local electric field divided by its positive eigenmass as the pseudoscalar on the propagating mass shell. An additive constant in a potential reconstructed by integrating a vector has no matrix element of this field. The classical background equations, the variational proof and both three-point representations are written out here.

**Memory used to select the question.** The programme recorded a possible odd excitation of a four-dimensional colour flux tube. It suggested choosing this representation of the two transverse displacements. No remembered colour-tube frequency, anomaly coefficient or scattering fit sets a potential, a boundary condition, a charge or an entry in Section 9. The 1990 note [1] sets the convention in which we express the answer. The 1988 scalar-curvature example [8] supplied a reason to separate physical poles from coefficients in a derivative expansion.

**Results supported here by calculation.** The sign-definite flux overlap, its equality to the current projection, the charge-independent product at specified $\beta$, and the critical equal-strength scalar partner are derived above. Equation $M^{12}$ on p. 518 of [4] already contains the radial flux overlap, written as $4\pi\sum W^0\int\rho\,\varphi_l(H'+H/\rho)\,d\rho$. With the mode amplitude restored it gives $2b_0\mathcal I$. Here we normalize the propagating state and use the overlap to compute its two-phonon residue. This describes the evidence in this paper and makes no claim of first publication. The transversely localized vector itself has a documented period precedent. In the critical equal-strength statement both the mass degeneracy and equality of *normalized* residues matter.

**Retrospective bibliographic note, outside the 1991 argument.** Subsequent work on the longitudinal excitation treats nonlinear backreaction and radiation. A later study of flux tubes also discusses pseudoscalars in the minimal Abelian Higgs model and the comparison with Yang–Mills. Their existence reinforces the need to distinguish historical priority, binding of a linear excitation and its decay. Neither their numerical inputs nor any later lattice measurement enter (1)–(31). [Arodź and Hadasz, 1995 preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/9506021) · [Gaikwad, 2026 thesis](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/7ms0x3br)

At critical coupling the even mode realizes the scalar-curvature model of [8]. For canonical phonons, the magnitude of the ordered on-shell vertex of $q h_s R$ is $2|q|(p\cdot q_2)^2/\sigma$, where $q_2$ is the second phonon momentum. Comparing with (28) and using $|p\cdot q_2|=m_*^2/2$ gives $|q|=|N'(0)|/m_*^2=|Q_{\rm pole}|$. The resulting charge-independent product is $0.59747$. The approximate colour-tube inputs $0.37$ and $1.85$ registered in idea 013 give $0.68$ for the corresponding product. These theories have different flux and particle content. P04 remains open.

The computed longitudinal mass and the critical shape spectrum have precedents. The spectral factorization leading to (26) also appears in the BPS vortex literature. The contribution examined here is the equality of the two *polarized residues* with canonical fields. Priority for the odd cubic coupling has not been surveyed. [Goodband and Hindmarsh (1995)](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-ph/9503457) · [Agia (2015)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03404) · [Alonso-Izquierdo, García Fuertes and Mateos Guilarte (2016)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.09084)

**Normalization of the later curvature coefficient.** Equations (3), (16), (27) and (29) of Aurrekoetxea et al. use $\eta=\sqrt2 v$, $e\eta=m_V$, $\alpha_{\rm bg}=a$ and a worldsheet kinetic term $-(\partial\chi)^2/2$. With the overall dimensionless action convention $e=1$, the unit-plane-norm profiles are $(\varphi,a_{\rm mode})=(fu,-u')/\mu$, up to their common sign. Indeed (11) gives $2\pi\int\rho(f^2u^2+u'^2)\,d\rho=\mu^2$. Substitution in their (27) gives

$$
 |\kappa|=\frac{\pi}{\mu}\int_0^\infty
 [\rho^2 f'f u-\rho a'u']\,d\rho
 =\frac{2\pi}{\mu}\int_0^\infty a'u\,d\rho
 =\frac{\alpha}{\mu}=1.20101=0.38230\pi .
$$

The surface term vanishes at both ends. The reduction uses $\rho f'=(1-a)f$ and $a''-a'/\rho=-f^2(1-a)$ for the positive unit vortex. Thus the **integral** in their (27) and the canonical action (29) agree with the pole match; the **number** printed beside that integral, $-0.7646\pi$, has twice its magnitude. Reversing the mode sign only changes the sign. Doubling the eigenfunction would reproduce the number but quadruple its kinetic norm. Their stated conventions settle the canonical coefficient, while the origin of the printed numerical value cannot be inferred from those equations. [Aurrekoetxea et al., Sec.
